# The Three-Track Package

A submittal arrives as a set of documents. Somebody has to read them, in some order, and form a view. How that set is arranged is not administration — it is the first argument the applicant makes, because it tells the reviewer what the applicant thinks is load-bearing.

The arrangement this corpus uses is three tracks with a guide in front of them. Fifteen or so documents, plus the transmittal, plus the candidate work products that close the stated gaps.

## What the tracks are

**Track A, orientation and transmittal.** The formal cover letter, the reading guide, the introduction that establishes the method — design basis, design-basis accident, the extension to deliberate military action, the four characteristics — and the regulatory case that maps the extension onto the existing framework. A reviewer who reads Track A and nothing else should be able to say what is being claimed and under what authority.

**Track B, the core design basis.** The facility-class instrument itself, which is the artifact under review, plus the four instruments it inherits from: the source-term treatment, the multi-module coping adequacy basis, the defense-in-depth methodology, and the Command Broker nuclear annex.

**Track C, supporting instruments.** The use case, the uncrewed-systems threat basis and its nuclear annex, the accident description and trigger and monitoring layers, the transition architecture, the physical-protection assessment, the cyber treatment, and the assessment framework application guide.

## Why core is core

The distinction between Track B and Track C is the one worth getting right, because it is not a judgment about importance.

**Track B instruments are core because the governing instrument inherits from them.** The facility-class design basis does not restate the source-term argument; it names it and builds on it. Its conclusions are therefore not independently checkable without the inherited instrument in hand — a reviewer who has only the facility-class document has an argument with a hole where its foundation should be.

**Track C instruments are supporting because they are referenced rather than relied upon in that structural way.** They inform, they substantiate, they provide the threat basis and the worked examples. A reviewer can read and largely evaluate the governing instrument without them, then reach for them when a particular claim needs its backing.

Getting this wrong in either direction costs something real. Demote an inherited instrument to Track C and a reviewer may not read it before forming a view on an argument that depends on it. Promote a supporting instrument to Track B and the core swells until it stops functioning as a core, which is the more common failure.

The practical test: **if removing the instrument leaves a claim in the governing document unsupported rather than merely less well illustrated, it is core.**

## The Reviewer's Guide

The guide is the entry point, and its job is to impose a reading order on a set that would otherwise be approached alphabetically or by whatever landed first.

It states what each document contributes, what depends on what, and the sequence that makes the argument comprehensible. That sequence is not the order the documents were written, and it is usually not the order of formal importance either. It is the order in which the argument becomes followable.

A guide is real or decorative, and the difference is easy to spot. A real one tells a reviewer what they will not understand if they skip something. A decorative one lists the contents with a sentence of description each, which a table of contents already does.

## Currency across a version-controlled set

A package assembled at a moment in time holds copies of instruments at the versions current then. The working copies move on. This is normal for a set that is version-controlled as a whole, and it is also exactly how stale citations propagate — the package's own copy of an instrument can be several revisions behind the live one while the transmittal asserts it is the current basis.

Two disciplines keep it honest. The package carries a **version index** that says what version of each instrument it contains, so a reviewer can tell at a glance whether they are reading current material. And any document that cites an instrument cites it **by designator with the version resolved in one place**, which is the rule this site applies through its [Instrument Library](../../resources/instrument-library.md) and which a submittal benefits from equally.

The failure this prevents is specific and embarrassing: a reviewer finds the governing instrument citing a version that the package does not contain, and now has a question about the whole set that has nothing to do with the technical case.

## Where the candidate work products sit

The twelve work products are not a fourth track. They are the vehicles named by the gaps the package states, and they sit alongside it rather than inside it — some closing a Commission-directed finding, some supplying standard submittal components, some strengthening a technical argument that will be pressed.

Their relationship to the package is the subject of the closing unit of this module. The thing to note here is structural: **a package that states gaps and a set of products that close them are two halves of one filing posture**, and separating them in the arrangement makes that posture visible rather than implied.

## What a reviewer should press on

Whether the core set matches what the governing instrument actually inherits — checkable by reading the governing instrument's own inheritance statements against the Track B list, and the fastest way to find out whether the arrangement was thought about.

Whether the Reviewer's Guide tells them what they will misunderstand if they read out of order, or merely lists what is there.

And whether the package's version index agrees with the instruments it contains.

## What to carry forward

Three tracks with a guide in front: orientation and transmittal, core design basis, supporting instruments. Core is core because the governing instrument *inherits* from it, not because it is important — and the test is whether removing it leaves a claim unsupported. The guide earns its place by imposing a reading order and saying what breaks without it. A version index and cite-by-designator keep a version-controlled set from propagating stale citations.
