# LI-02 — Campus-Scaled Critical Digital Asset Identification

Cyber-security rules for reactors work by first deciding which digital equipment matters. You identify the assets whose compromise could hurt safety, security, or emergency preparedness, and you protect those to a high standard. The method was built when a site meant one reactor, or two. A small modular campus may run a dozen modules sharing a single control building — and an asset that is important for one module and *critical for all twelve at once* does not sit comfortably anywhere in the existing scoping method.

**The issue** — Critical-digital-asset identification issues a single designation with no gradation, so a shared asset whose compromise is consequential across many modules is classified and protected exactly as one serving a single module.

**Where it surfaces** — DBA-MA-SMR-FC1 §7.4, the first of three gaps that section states; §9.5 folds all three into one undifferentiated ask. Candidate work product 1B decomposes them and routes this one.

**Regulatory hook** — 10 CFR 73.54, implemented through the endorsed industry scoping guidance and the corresponding regulatory guide.

**Why it is open** — **Guidance is absent.** The requirement in 73.54 is perfectly capable of covering a campus, and so is the scoping method: the shared asset is unambiguously critical and no argument is needed to make it so. What does not exist is any way for the method to record that it is critical across twelve units rather than one, or any instruction about what follows from that. The guidance contemplates a single asset serving several critical systems only where those systems perform *different* regulated functions — safety and emergency preparedness, for instance — which is multiplicity of function, not of unit. The nearest analogue in the document points away from the campus case.

**Closure vehicle and owner** — An update to the industry critical-digital-asset scoping guidance and its endorsing regulatory guide. **Owner: the Nuclear Energy Institute**, developing it, with the Commission endorsing. This is an industry-led item by design: the endorsement mechanism exists precisely so that method-level questions of this kind are worked in the consensus process and then accepted by reference, rather than resolved case by case in twelve separate reviews.

**State of play** — *As of September 2026.* Open. Candidate 1B is drafted at v0.1 and states the routing; the corpus has not taken this to the consensus process.

The entry was restated in September 2026 after the scoping guidance was read at source rather than characterized from secondary description, and the restatement matters to how the ask should be framed. Two corrections came out of it. The campus asset is not missed by the method, so an ask premised on an absence invites the answer that the existing rule already covers it. And inclusion by connectivity is conditional and dischargeable rather than transitive — a device that merely communicates with a critical asset is not critical for that reason alone — so the population problem the corpus had assumed does not arise in the form assumed. The defensible ask is narrower and more buildable than the one previously stated: a gradation in the designation, and an instruction about what follows from it. See [M04-CN-03](../resources/course-notes/M04-CN-03-identifying-critical-digital-assets.md). The site continues to characterize the guidance rather than reproduce it, as copyright requires.

A second, practical thread runs alongside. The guidance already permits grouping several devices, or a whole network, into a single critical asset where the licensee justifies that the plan's protective requirements are satisfied for that configuration. That is the mechanism a campus applicant would reach for, and it exists today. What is undefined is the standard such a justification must meet when the configuration spans twelve licensed units. An applicant proposing to group at campus scale should supply that standard rather than wait to be asked for it.

## Reader's stake

*For the practitioner* — How this is stated decides whether it survives contact with a reviewer. Stated as "the method does not reach campus configurations," it is wrong and will be corrected, because the method reaches them perfectly well. Stated as "the method issues one designation where the configuration needs two," it names something specific, true, and within the consensus process's power to build. The second framing also has a fallback the first does not: the grouping provision already in the guidance, used with a justification standard the applicant proposes.

*For everyone else* — The rules for protecting reactor computers work out which computers matter and protect those. On a site with a dozen small reactors sharing equipment, a shared computer plainly matters — that much is not in doubt. What the rules have no way of saying is that it matters twelve times more than a computer serving one reactor, so it gets the same label and the same protection either way. The gap is not that nobody noticed the shared computer. It is that the label has only one setting.
