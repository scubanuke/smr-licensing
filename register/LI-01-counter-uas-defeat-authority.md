# LI-01 — Counter-UAS Defeat Authority

A drone over a nuclear plant can be seen and tracked. It cannot lawfully be brought down. The licensee has the sensors, the trained guard force, and the obligation to prevent radiological sabotage — and no authority to act on what the sensors show. A federal military installation a few miles away has both. That gap is not an oversight in the plant's security plan; it is a gap in statute, and no amount of regulatory compliance closes it.

**The issue** — A commercial reactor licensee has no lawful authority to actively defeat an uncrewed aircraft over its protected area, while bearing a regulatory obligation to protect against radiological sabotage that such an aircraft could enable.

**Where it surfaces** — DBA-MA-SMR-FC1 §7.5 raises it and §9.6 carries it as a deliberate open pass. The threat basis behind it is in DBA-UxS and its nuclear annex. Candidate work product 1A develops the closure argument.

**Regulatory hook** — 10 CFR Part 73 generally, and 73.55 for physical protection. The absent authority sits outside Title 10 altogether, alongside the Section 1602 counter-uncrewed-systems authority granted to federal departments, with airspace deconfliction reserved to the Federal Aviation Administration under 49 U.S.C. 40103.

**Why it is open** — **Authority is missing.** The Commission cannot grant by regulation a power Congress has not conferred. This is the cleanest example on the register of a question that looks regulatory and is not.

**Closure vehicle and owner** — A targeted statutory extension of the existing federal counter-uncrewed-systems authority to NRC-licensed power reactors, exercised within Part 73 and deconflicted by the FAA. **Owner: Congress**, with the Commission able to write implementing physical-protection requirements only once the authority exists, and the licensee writing site concept-of-operations below that. The corpus position, argued in 1A, is that the authority should be grounded in the intent-agnostic sabotage-prevention mission rather than in any determination that an act of war has occurred — which separates the *existence* of the authority, standing at all times, from the *posture* at which it is exercised.

**State of play** — *As of September 2026.* Open. Two asymmetries remain unaddressed: detect-but-not-defeat for the licensee, and federal-site-yes against commercial-reactor-no across otherwise comparable consequence. Candidate 1A is drafted at v0.1 and proposes the vehicle; it has not been placed with a sponsor.

## Reader's stake

*For the practitioner* — This is one of two findings a Safety Evaluation cannot close on the staff's own authority, which makes how it is presented a live drafting decision. A submittal that treats it as a licensee performance gap invites a finding the applicant cannot satisfy; one that states it as a statutory boundary, with the vehicle named, hands the staff something it can record honestly and elevate.

*For everyone else* — If you have wondered why plant operators talk about drones so carefully, this is why. The people responsible for the plant are allowed to watch and are not allowed to act, and the rule that produces that result was written for airspace safety rather than for reactor protection. Fixing it takes an act of Congress, not a better security plan.
