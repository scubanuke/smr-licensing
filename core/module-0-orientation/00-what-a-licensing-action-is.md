# What a Licensing Action Is

Someone wants to build a reactor. A federal agency decides whether they may. Everything else in this subject is the shape that decision has taken over seventy years of trying to make it defensible.

The decision is not a permission slip. It is a **written finding**, made on a record, that there is reasonable assurance of adequate protection of public health and safety. Four things follow from that phrasing and they are worth taking slowly.

It is a **finding**, so it must have a basis, and the basis has to be in the record rather than in the reviewer's judgment. It is **written**, so it can be read, quoted, and disputed. It is a standard of **reasonable assurance** rather than certainty, because certainty is not available and a regime that pretended otherwise would be dishonest about what engineering can deliver. And it concerns **adequate protection**, a term of art that means what is required irrespective of cost.

## The chain

An **application** is submitted. It is **docketed**, which makes it a formal, mostly public record with a number. The staff conducts a **review**, asking questions in writing where the record does not support a conclusion. The staff writes a **safety evaluation** setting out its findings. An independent committee looks at the safety case and the evaluation and writes its own view. A **hearing** process runs, which interested parties may seek to join. And a decision issues.

Module 2 covers what happens inside the review. The point here is the shape: a written claim, tested in public, concluded in writing.

## Who can move what

The most useful thing to know about the parties is not what they are called but **what each one can actually change**. Almost every question on this site's register turns on that, and a reader who has this list can work out for themselves who a given problem belongs to.

**The applicant** can change the design, the analysis, the application, and what they commit to. They cannot change what is required of them.

**The NRC staff** reviews, asks, and writes the finding. They can interpret requirements, issue guidance, and endorse industry methods. They cannot grant an authority that Congress has not conferred, and they do not set the design-basis threat.

**The Advisory Committee on Reactor Safeguards** reviews the safety case and the staff's evaluation independently and reports to the Commission in public letters. It can disagree, sharply, and does. It does not decide.

**The Commission** — five appointees — sets policy, issues rules, defines the design-basis threat, and decides matters that come before it. This is where a question goes when it needs an answer the staff cannot give on its own authority.

**The Nuclear Energy Institute**, acting for industry, develops guidance through a consensus process which the Commission may then endorse, at which point it becomes an accepted way of complying. Industry can move method. Industry cannot move the threat basis, because the threat basis is not industry's to set.

**Intervenors** — members of the public, states, organizations — may seek to participate in the hearing on contentions that must first be admitted. They can force an issue onto the record and make it be answered.

**Congress** writes statute, which is where an authority comes from when nobody in the list above has it.

Keep that list. When you reach the register, the closure-vehicle field on every entry names one of these parties, and the entries that feel most intractable are the ones whose owner is furthest from the room — [LI-01](../../register/LI-01-counter-uas-defeat-authority.md) needs Congress, and no amount of excellent work by anyone else will close it.

## Two regulatory worlds

Safety and security are reviewed under different parts of Title 10, against different criteria, historically by different reviewers.

Safety asks what happens when things fail. Security asks what happens when somebody makes them fail. They are not the same question and the difference is not one of severity — an adversary chooses, and choosing is a property no failure has.

Keeping them distinct is why this site has a separate security module, and why [Module 4](../module-4-security-licensing/README.md) spends its time on a threat construct rather than on hazards. The seam between the two worlds is itself governed, which turns out to be a precedent that matters later.

## Why a small modular reactor is not a small large reactor

Four differences, and all four are licensing differences before they are engineering ones.

**A campus rather than a unit.** Many reactors on one site sharing infrastructure. Almost every hard question on this site's register is a version of *what does sharing do to a demonstration built for a single unit*.

**Factory fabrication.** Modules built in a plant and delivered, which moves quality assurance upstream into a supply chain and changes what inspection means.

**A consequence-scaled planning zone.** Potentially much smaller than the familiar ring, because the consequence calculation supports it — Module 3.

**Siting near load.** Which is the commercial point of the smaller zone, and the beginning of the most uncomfortable argument in the corpus.

None of those follow from the reactor being small. They follow from what people intend to do with small reactors, which is why they are licensing problems.

## What to carry forward

A licensing action ends in a written finding of reasonable assurance of adequate protection, made on a mostly public record. The parties are best understood by what each can change, and that list is what makes the register's closure-vehicle field legible. Safety and security are separate regulatory worlds with a governed seam between them. And a small modular reactor differs from a small large reactor in four ways that are all consequences of how people intend to use it.
