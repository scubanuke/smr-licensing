# Safeguards Information

Reactor licensing happens in public. An application is docketed, the staff's evaluation is written down, the Advisory Committee's views are recorded, and a neighbor who wants to know what was claimed and what was found can go and read it. That is not ceremonial. It is the mechanism by which a decision made by a federal agency about a hazard in somebody's community earns the standing to be accepted.

Some of what a thorough security analysis contains would be useful to an attacker. Both of those things are true at once, and the question of where exactly to draw the line between them cannot be answered by instinct or by deferring to whoever is most cautious in the room.

## Two protection regimes

The category most of this material falls into is **Safeguards Information**, governed by 10 CFR 73.21 and 73.22. It is a designation of its own, distinct from classified national-security information: security-sensitive material that is withheld from the public record, with rules about who may have access, what they must do to be eligible, how it must be marked, stored, transmitted and destroyed, and what happens if it is disclosed. It typically covers things like physical-protection plan detail, response strategies, and the specifics of target sets — the operational particulars of how a given site is defended.

Alongside it sits the **classified** regime, which reaches material derived from intelligence sources. Threat characterizations that come from the intelligence community are the usual case, and they carry a different and heavier apparatus. The distinction matters for this corpus because the military-action extension touches material whose natural home is the second regime rather than the first, which is part of why [LI-11](../../register/LI-11-the-warning-pathway-and-who-decides.md) and [LI-13](../../register/LI-13-geopolitical-siting.md) are as difficult as they are.

## The tension, stated honestly

The straightforward version of the problem is that protecting security-sensitive detail is obviously right and the only question is scope. That version is incomplete, and the incompleteness is where the real risk lives.

**Over-protection is a failure mode, not a safe default.** If the uncomfortable parts of a safety case can be moved into a protected annex, then the public record shows a design that looks better than the design is. Everything reassuring stays visible; every declared residual, every conditional claim, every gap the analysts were honest enough to write down goes behind the wall. Nobody has to decide to mislead anyone for this to happen — each individual classification call can be defensible while the aggregate effect is a record that no longer supports the trust placed in it.

That risk bears directly on the method this corpus is built around. The whole point of the Conditionality Check in [Module 5](../module-5-military-action-layer/01-the-conditionality-check.md) is to declare a residual rather than let it stay implicit. A residual declared into a Safeguards annex has been declared to the people who already knew and withheld from the people who carry it.

Under-protection is the more obvious failure and needs less arguing. Site-specific detail about how a particular plant is defended, and where its defense is thinnest, does not belong on a public docket.

## The mechanism: class and derivative

The corpus's answer is not a judgment call applied document by document but a structural one, and it is the reason the whole instrument architecture is shaped the way it is.

**Class-level instruments are site-neutral and stay public.** They state general findings and methods: how a facility class behaves, what barriers it credits, what the analysis method is, where the gaps are. They inform design without targeting any particular plant, because there is no particular plant in them.

**Derivatives accumulate the site-specific detail and are designated before docketing.** Vulnerability assessment for a given site, hardening parameters, discrimination criteria, the specifics of a correlated event at an actual campus — these arise only when a class-level method is applied to a real location, which is exactly the point at which the material becomes actionable.

So the class-versus-derivative resolution split *is* the classification-management device. It is not a filing convention that happens to have a security benefit; it is the mechanism, and it works because it sorts material at the moment of authorship rather than at the moment of filing.

The test underneath it is a **material test**: would this passage materially aid an attack on, or the defeat of, a specific facility? Methodology, architecture, stated gaps and conformance matrices pass and stay public. Actionable site-specific detail does not. Applying that test honestly is considerably harder than stating it, which is why the corpus's own treatment names specific review points — the uncrewed-systems vulnerability profiles, the seizure analysis, the campus cyber material — as places where general treatment shades toward actionable and each passage has to be read against the test individually rather than by category.

## What this does not settle

The classification and docket-handling work product is explicit that it is **not itself a classification determination** and recommends a formal review by the authorities that actually hold that power before anything is docketed. That is the right posture and it is worth understanding why: classification authority is not something a corpus can assume, and a document that behaved as though it had would be both wrong and unhelpful.

What an author can do is structural, and it is most of the value. Write class instruments to be public by construction. Push site-specific detail into derivatives from the start. Treat every derivative as a protection-review object by default. Do that and the eventual formal review is a confirmation rather than a rewrite. Leave it until filing and the choice is between a bad disclosure and an expensive re-authoring.

The open form of all this is [LI-12](../../register/LI-12-public-docket-and-safeguards-split.md).

## What a reviewer should press on

Whether any declared residual, conditionality statement, or admitted gap has migrated out of the public record — the single most important question in this unit, and the one least likely to be asked.

Whether the class and derivative boundary was applied at authorship or retrofitted.

And whether a formal classification and Safeguards review is scheduled before docketing, rather than assumed to have happened somewhere.

## What to carry forward

Two regimes: Safeguards Information under 73.21 and 73.22, and classified national-security information for intelligence-derived material. Over-protection is a real failure mode because it hollows out the public record while leaving it reassuring. The class-versus-derivative split is the mechanism that manages the boundary, applied when the material is written rather than when it is filed. The test is whether a passage would materially aid an attack on a specific facility — and the method and the admitted gaps stay public even where the numbers do not.
