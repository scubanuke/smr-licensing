# Topical Reports

A topical report is a document submitted for review on its own, not as part of any particular application, addressing one subject — a method, a component qualification, an analytical approach. The staff reviews it and, if satisfied, issues a safety evaluation saying so. From then on, applicants can reference it instead of re-arguing the subject.

Stated that way it sounds administrative. It is the single most important economic mechanism in this module, and understanding why explains most of the form the design-basis corpus takes.

## The capital argument

A first-of-a-kind project carries regulatory cost that a repeat project does not: the analyses nobody has done before, the arguments nobody has made, the review time nobody has spent. For a conventional programme that cost is amortized across a fleet. For the first one it is carried alone.

If that cost is **sunk**, it is a straightforward burden on the first mover and a reason to wait for someone else to go first — which, when everyone reasons that way, is why nothing gets built.

If it is **referable**, it becomes something else. The first applicant to get a method reviewed and accepted has created an asset: later applicants can reference the accepted method, which lowers their cost and shortens their schedule. The first mover's regulatory spending turns into a position rather than a loss, and a vendor selling a design that comes with reviewed topical reports is selling something materially more valuable than the same design without them.

That is why this is a capital argument rather than a procedural one, and why a technical author should care. Writing to topical-report standard is not paperwork discipline. It is the difference between an argument that has to be made again and an argument that has been settled.

## What writing to that standard requires

Three things a document needs if it is to be referable, and their absence is what makes an otherwise excellent technical document unusable as a topical report.

**An applicability statement.** What the report covers, what it does not, and the conditions under which its conclusions hold. A later applicant needs to determine whether their case falls inside the envelope, and a reviewer needs to determine the same thing. A report without a crisp applicability statement cannot be referenced safely by anyone, because nobody can tell whether it applies.

**Pathway selection.** Which framework the report is written against, since a requirement answered under one may not exist in the same form under another.

**A conformance matrix.** The requirement-by-requirement mapping from Module 2, which is the spine the safety evaluation is built on.

The corpus's own wrapper work product supplies exactly these three for the facility-class instrument, which was written to topical-report standard without carrying them. That is a common situation: the technical substance is done to the right standard and the referability apparatus is missing, and supplying it is a comparatively small piece of work with a disproportionate effect.

## Conditions and limitations

A safety evaluation on a topical report usually comes with **conditions and limitations** — the report is accepted, provided certain things hold, within stated bounds.

These matter more than they look. They travel with the report. An applicant referencing it inherits them and must demonstrate their own case satisfies each one, and a reference that ignores a condition is not a valid reference. Reading a topical report's safety evaluation without reading its conditions is one of the more reliable ways to build an application on a foundation that is not there.

## The width problem

There is a genuine tension in scoping one, and it has no clean answer.

**Too narrow** and the report covers so specific a case that few later applicants fall inside it. The review cost was incurred and the asset is small.

**Too broad** and it becomes unreviewable — the staff cannot reach a defensible conclusion across an envelope wide enough to include cases nobody has analyzed, so the review stalls or the acceptance arrives with conditions so extensive that referencing it is no easier than starting over.

The judgment is to scope to a **class**: wide enough that a meaningful population of later applications falls inside, narrow enough that the argument genuinely holds across the whole envelope. That is precisely what a facility class is, and it is why this corpus organizes around facility classes rather than around individual plants or around the whole sector. The class is the unit at which an argument can be both true and reusable.

Which also explains the class-versus-derivative split that reappears in Module 6 as the classification mechanism. It was a referability structure before it was a protection one, and it turns out to serve both.

## What a reviewer should press on

Whether the applicability statement is precise enough to determine inclusion, or whether it describes the subject rather than bounding it.

Whether conditions and limitations from any referenced report have been carried through and demonstrated, rather than cited and dropped.

And whether the class the report is scoped to is one the argument actually holds across — the failure being a report scoped to a class for commercial reach, with an argument that only works for the subset the author had in mind.

## What to carry forward

Reviewed once, referable thereafter — which converts first-of-a-kind regulatory cost from a sunk burden into an asset, and is why writing to topical-report standard is an economic decision rather than a documentation one. Referability needs an applicability statement, a pathway selection and a conformance matrix. Conditions and limitations travel with the report and bind whoever references it. And the scoping judgment is to write to a *class*: wide enough to be worth referencing, narrow enough that the argument holds across it.
