# Part 50 and Part 52

There are two established ways to get permission to build and run a power reactor in the United States, and the difference between them is not procedural detail. It is about *when* the regulator's questions get answered, and that turns out to decide whether large capital projects are financeable at all.

## The two-step route

The original structure, in Part 50, has two permissions. A **construction permit** authorizes building. Years later, with the plant substantially complete, the licensee applies for an **operating licence**, and that application is reviewed and can be contested.

Read as a safety architecture this is sensible: you learn a great deal during construction, and reviewing the operating application with the plant in front of you means reviewing what was actually built rather than what was proposed.

Read as an investment structure it is close to intolerable. A utility spends billions on construction with permission to operate still outstanding, and the operating-licence proceeding can reopen questions that were assumed settled at the permit stage. The financial exposure sits precisely where the project cannot walk away from it. That dynamic, more than any single technical problem, is what ended the first wave of United States reactor construction — the late projects that were abandoned mostly died of schedule and cost risk, and a large part of that risk was regulatory and back-loaded.

## What Part 52 changed

Part 52 moved the questions forward. The aim is to resolve as much as possible **before** construction begins, so the licensee is not spending capital against an open decision. It does that with three instruments that can be used separately or together.

**Design certification** approves a reactor design generically, by rulemaking, independent of any site. The certified design becomes a rule that later applicants reference rather than re-litigate. This is where the certified quantities of Module 3 come from and why they are carried by reference.

**An early site permit** approves a site — its characteristics, hazards, and emergency-planning suitability — before an applicant has chosen a design or committed to build. Site questions and design questions are hard enough separately; resolving them separately is most of the value.

**A combined licence** authorizes construction and, conditionally, operation, in a single proceeding. That conditionality is the crux, and it needs its own mechanism.

## ITAAC, and what "conditionally" means

If operating authority is granted before the plant exists, something has to guarantee the plant that eventually exists is the one that was approved. That mechanism is **ITAAC** — inspections, tests, analyses and acceptance criteria.

A combined licence carries a set of them: verifiable statements of what will be checked during and after construction, and what result counts as acceptable. As construction proceeds the licensee demonstrates each one has been met. Before fuel load, the Commission must find that all of them are satisfied, and that finding is the gate.

The effect is to convert the old back-loaded discretionary review into a front-loaded specified one. The licensee still has to prove the plant is right; but they know in advance what proving it consists of, and a satisfied criterion is not reopened. That is the difference between a risk you can price and one you cannot.

ITAAC is worth understanding because it is the clearest instance of a pattern that recurs throughout this subject: **specify the test in advance and the demonstration becomes tractable.** The admissibility gate in Module 5 is the same move applied to machine-learning provenance, and the conformance matrix in Module 2 is the same move applied to the regulation as a whole.

## What a combined licence does not do

Two things, worth stating because the name oversells.

It does not remove the hearing process. Interested parties may still seek to participate, and contentions may still be litigated. What it changes is *when*, which is the point.

And it does not make the ITAAC stage a formality. Demonstrating that criteria have been met is real work, and a criterion drafted vaguely at the licensing stage becomes an argument at the construction stage, when arguments are most expensive. Applicants who treat ITAAC drafting as boilerplate discover this late.

## Which route, and why it matters here

Near-term small modular reactor projects have generally worked in the Part 52 world, and the reasons are structural rather than preferential. A modular design is intended to be built repeatedly, which is exactly what certification is for. A first-of-a-kind capital case cannot carry back-loaded regulatory risk. And a design intended to be sited near load benefits from resolving site questions on their own timetable.

That structural fit is also why the topical-report mechanism in this module's third unit matters so much for this class of reactor: it is the same logic — resolve once, reference thereafter — applied to individual technical arguments rather than to a whole design.

The modern technology-inclusive framework, and where a given design sits relative to it, is the next unit.

## What a reviewer should press on

Whether the pathway is stated explicitly and early, with the reason for the choice, rather than left inferable from the application's structure.

Whether the ITAAC are drafted as verifiable statements with unambiguous acceptance criteria, or as intentions.

And whether what is being incorporated by reference — a certified design, an approved site, a topical report — is identified precisely enough that a reviewer can pull it and check.

## What to carry forward

Part 50's two-step route reviews operation after construction, which back-loads regulatory risk onto capital that cannot walk away. Part 52 moves the questions forward through design certification, early site permits and the combined licence, with ITAAC as the mechanism that makes conditional operating authority meaningful. The recurring pattern is *specify the test in advance and the demonstration becomes tractable*. And a combined licence does not remove hearings or make ITAAC a formality.
